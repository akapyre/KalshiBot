Is Your Vision At Risk On GLP's?

You've probably seen the headlines claiming Ozempic and other GLP-1s cause blindness; at least during my last stream, you did. I went through the literature so you don't have to. Short version: there's a real but small statistical signal, strongest for semaglutide. Europe now lists it as a "very rare" side effect, but causation has NOT been proven, and the mechanism is still unknown. Here's a full breakdown.

NAION (non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy) is basically a "stroke" of the optic nerve caused by reduced blood flow. It shows up as sudden, painless vision loss, usually in one eye, often noticed on waking. It's the most common cause of acute optic nerve damage in adults over 50, affecting roughly 2 to 10 per 100,000 people over 50 each year. The vision loss is usually permanent, and there's no proven treatment.

THE KEY STUDIES

Hathaway et al. (JAMA Ophthalmology, 2024): This is the study that kicked everything off. It came from a single neuro-ophthalmology center at Mass Eye and Ear. It found a 4.28x higher risk in diabetics and a 7.64x higher risk in overweight/obese patients on semaglutide. Big numbers, but it was a small referral population, which inflates risk estimates.

Grauslund et al. (Denmark, 2024): This nationwide cohort covered 424,152 people with type 2 diabetes. Semaglutide was associated with roughly DOUBLE the 5-year risk of NAION. Only 218 total cases occurred across about 1.9 million person-years.

Chou et al. (Ophthalmology, 2025): This multinational matched study of about 18,600 diabetics found NO statistically significant increase in risk.

Hsu et al. (TriNetX, 2025): This large, diverse US database found about 2 to 2.4x higher risk at 2, 3, and 4 years.

American Journal of Ophthalmology (2025): This large matched US cohort found NO risk difference for semaglutide or any GLP-1 vs. controls. The 5-year incidence stayed under 0.1%.

JAMA Network Open (2025): This was the first major study including tirzepatide, with about 159,000 matched diabetics. Over 2 years, 0.04% of sema/tirz users developed NAION vs. 0.02% of controls (about 1.76x).

Nogueira et al. (Diabetes Care meta-analysis, January 2026): This pooled 15 studies with over 1.5 million patients. GLP-1 use was associated with about 1.7x higher odds of NAION. The absolute risk difference was only 0.037%, meaning roughly 2,700 people would need to take a GLP-1 for one extra case to occur. Overall eye problems did not increase.

Li et al. (Diabetes Care meta-analysis of RCTs, 2026): This pooled 20 randomized controlled trials with 83,000+ participants and found NO significant association with ischemic optic neuropathy or vision loss. The caveat is that these trials weren't designed to look for NAION specifically.

The pattern: most observational studies show about a 1.5 to 2x relative risk increase, several show nothing, and the gold-standard randomized trial data show no significant signal.

WHAT REGULATORS AND EXPERTS SAY

NAION was officially classified as a "very rare" side effect of semaglutide, affecting up to 1 in 10,000 users. That works out to about 1 extra case per 10,000 person-years of treatment. The EMA says to stop semaglutide if NAION is confirmed. The WHO issued an alert following this decision.

Novo Nordisk: The company maintains the evidence does not support a causal relationship and that the benefit-risk profile remains favorable.

United States: As of recent reporting, NAION is not on the US labels for Ozempic, Wegovy, or Rybelsus. The FDA is reportedly still evaluating. There's also active litigation over this.

HOW COULD A GLP-1 CAUSE THIS? (ALL HYPOTHESES, NONE PROVEN)

The setup: most people who get NAION have what's called a "disc at risk." That means a small, crowded optic disc with little or no central cup. When blood flow drops, the nerve fibers swell, but there's no room for them to expand. That swelling squeezes the capillaries, which cuts blood flow further, and the cycle kills nerve cells. Any GLP-1 effect is likely a "second hit" on an eye that's already structurally vulnerable.

Theory 1, blood pressure drops: GLP-1s lower blood pressure, and low blood pressure at night is a known NAION trigger. Viagra and Cialis (PDE-5 inhibitors) are linked to NAION through a similar mechanism. Add reduced food and water intake and dehydration, and optic nerve perfusion could drop.

Theory 2, changing too fast: Rapid improvements in blood sugar are already known to temporarily worsen diabetic retinopathy. This "early worsening" was seen with semaglutide in the SUSTAIN-6 trial. Rapid weight loss, fluid shifts, and nutritional gaps could stress the optic nerve's blood supply in a similar way.

Theory 3, direct receptor effects: GLP-1 receptors exist in the retina and optic nerve. These drugs affect blood vessel tone and nitric oxide signaling, which could theoretically cause local blood flow problems in a vulnerable nerve. NIH-funded research at Harvard is currently looking at exactly this.

The skeptic's case: Several top neuro-ophthalmologists argue there's no biologically plausible mechanism. GLP-1s actually improve most NAION risk factors, including blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, and sleep apnea. The biggest problem with the research is confounding: the people prescribed GLP-1s (diabetic, obese, hypertensive, sleep apnea) are the same people already at high risk for NAION.

WHO IS HIGHER RISK?

* Small, crowded optic disc ("disc at risk")
* Age over 50
* Diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol
* Sleep apnea
* Low blood pressure at night
* Smoking, anemia, clotting disorders
* PDE-5 inhibitors (Cialis, Viagra) and amiodarone

Experts are most cautious about GLP-1s in people with prior NAION or vision loss in one eye, a crowded optic disc, sleep apnea, nocturnal hypotension, or significant vascular disease.

On tirzepatide, the data is very thin. In the only head-to-head comparison, there were 2 NAION cases on semaglutide and 1 on tirzepatide, which is far too few to draw conclusions either way. There is no NAION data on retatrutide at all.

Almost all of this research comes from people with diabetes or obesity using pharmacy-grade semaglutide. It doesn't directly tell us about leaner users, newer compounds like retatrutide, or research-grade products, where purity and dosing accuracy add their own unknowns.

1. If you're over 50 or have risk factors, get a baseline dilated eye exam. An eye doctor can tell you if you have a "disc at risk.
2. Stay hydrated, especially when appetite is suppressed. Don't rush titration.
3. Be careful stacking GLP-1s with high doses of Cialis/Viagra or blood pressure meds, since they all push blood flow in the same direction.
4. If you have prior NAION or vision loss in one eye, talk to a doctor before using these compounds.
5. Sudden vision loss in one eye is an EMERGENCY. Stop and get seen immediately. Don't wait it out.

This is not something the majority of you guys need to be freaking out about.
