# One Dose Of Liraglutide, Two Weeks Of Symptoms - A New Case Report

You've probably noticed that most of the GLP-1 safety talk focuses on people who are weeks or months deep into a protocol. A case report that just dropped goes the other direction, a FIRST dose, in a healthy young person, so let's talk about it.

WHAT THEY REPORTED

Published in Galen Medical Journal. A 30-year-old healthy female took a single 1 mg injection of liraglutide for weight loss, outside of a doctor's care. Within a few hours she had severe abdominal pain and persistent vomiting.

They worked her up with imaging and labs and found NOTHING structurally wrong to explain it. No obstruction, no other cause. The picture they describe is a severe gastroparesis-like syndrome, meaning the stomach basically stopped moving things along.

She recovered with supportive care over roughly two weeks and her symptoms resolved completely.

WHY THE STOMACH IS THE TARGET ORGAN HERE

This is the part people skip. Delayed gastric emptying isn't a weird side effect of GLP-1s, it's part of HOW they work.

Think of the exit of your stomach as a valve. GLP-1 receptor signaling taps the brake on that valve so food leaves slower. Slower emptying = you stay full longer = you eat less. That's a feature, not a bug.

The problem is when the brake gets pressed too hard. Then food just sits there, pressure builds, and you get the classic picture: pain, nausea, vomiting, feeling full off almost nothing.

And here's the piece that makes a first-dose report make sense. The gastric-emptying effect is strongest early and tends to fade with continued exposure as the gut adapts. Your very first exposure is when that brake has the most grip.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT PROVE

1. This is a CASE REPORT. n=1. No control group, no comparison, no way to calculate risk.
2. Case reports exist to flag that something CAN happen, not how often it happens. Millions of liraglutide doses have been given and this is being published precisely because it's unusual.
3. The authors themselves say more study is needed to explain these reactions and to set rules for safer GLP-1 use outside of diabetic populations.
4. Nothing in the report tells us anything about what she actually injected beyond the stated 1 mg, which in an unsupervised setting is its own unknown.

WHAT I ACTUALLY TAKE FROM IT

* Severe GI reactions on GLP-1s are usually framed as a titration problem. This is a reminder they can be a FIRST EXPOSURE problem too.
* "Healthy and young" is not a shield. She had no underlying condition they could find.
* Vomiting you cannot stop is not "normal GLP-1 nausea." Persistent vomiting with severe abdominal pain, unable to keep fluids down, is a go-get-seen situation, not a push-through situation.
* Recovery took two weeks of supportive care. Stopping the compound isn't an instant off switch when the half-life and the gut are both still working through it.

The realistic read: this is rare, it resolved fully, and one case doesn't change the risk picture for the average person. But it's a clean example of why the GI side of these compounds deserves respect from dose one, not just at the 5 mg mark.
