# Does BPC-157 Actually Have Human Evidence? A Literature Walk-Through

BPC-157 is permanently in the top three questions I get, and Cleveland Clinic just dropped a consumer piece on it this week, so let's talk about what the literature on this thing ACTUALLY contains, and what it doesn't.

I'm going to do this a little differently than usual. I'm not going to throw a wall of numbers at you, because almost every number you see quoted for BPC-157 online has been ripped out of a rodent paper and dressed up like it was a human trial. So instead, let's walk through the literature by TYPE, and you'll see the pattern yourself.

Quick background: BPC-157 is a 15-amino-acid sequence derived from a protein found in human gastric juice. "Body Protection Compound." The bulk of the published work traces back to one research group in Zagreb, Croatia, that has been publishing on it since the 1990s.

THE ANIMAL LITERATURE (where basically all of it lives)

1. Tendon and ligament models. Rats get a surgical transection of the Achilles tendon or a ligament, then get the peptide, usually by intraperitoneal injection or dosed in the drinking water, over days to a few weeks. Outcomes are things like load-to-failure on the healed tendon and histology of the repair tissue. This is the bucket that the "BPC heals tendons" claim comes from.

2. Gut models. Rats with induced colitis, NSAID-induced stomach damage, or fistulas. Given intragastrically or by injection. This is arguably the ORIGINAL use case, since the peptide was pulled out of gastric juice in the first place.

3. Nerve and vascular models. Sciatic nerve crush, spinal cord injury, vessel occlusion in rodents. The proposed mechanism here is upregulation of VEGF signaling and new blood vessel growth, basically building new plumbing into damaged tissue.

4. Cell culture. Fibroblast migration and growth assays in a dish. Useful for mechanism, tells you nothing about what happens in a human knee.

THE HUMAN LITERATURE

This is the short section, because there isn't one. There are no published, adequately powered, randomized placebo-controlled human trials showing BPC-157 heals tendon, ligament, or joint injury. Some early-phase work on gut indications exists in the registry world, but nothing that settles the question. Everything else people cite as "human evidence" is anecdote, clinic testimonials, or someone's podcast.

THE PATTERN

* Consistent, repeated positive results across many different rodent injury models
* Largely from a small number of affiliated research groups
* Administered in those studies by routes and in designs that don't map cleanly to what people do
* Essentially zero controlled human outcome data
* Regulatory reality: the FDA moved BPC-157 to Category 2 on its compounding bulk substances list in 2023, meaning it flagged significant safety concerns, and WADA has had it banned for athletes since 2022

Now compare that to a headline that also came out this week: a topical peptide spray outperformed placebo for healing burn wounds. That's the structure you WANT to see. A defined compound, a human population, a placebo arm, a measured endpoint. That's what "evidence" looks like when it exists. BPC-157 doesn't have that yet.

WHAT THIS ACTUALLY MEANS

It does NOT mean BPC-157 is fake. The preclinical signal is genuinely broad and genuinely interesting, and I think it's one of the more fascinating repair-signaling stories out there. What it means is that anyone telling you "studies show it heals your tendon in 4 weeks" is quoting a rat with a cut Achilles and hoping you don't check.

And this is exactly why the consumer-protection pieces on social media peptides keep showing up. The gap between "strong rodent data" and "proven in humans" is where almost all the marketing lives.

Hold both ideas at once: promising mechanism, thin human evidence. That's not a knock on the compound, that's just honesty about where we are. Drop your thoughts below.
