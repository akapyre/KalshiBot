# "Serious GLP-1 Side Effects" - Why Nobody Can Tell You How Often

You've probably seen the headline floating around this week: people on GLP-1s are having serious side effects, but it's unclear how often. That last part is the whole story, and almost nobody explains WHY it's unclear. So let's talk about it.

I'm not going to throw a bunch of percentages at you today, because the honest answer is that the denominator is missing. Instead I want to walk you through the actual tiers of evidence we have on GLP-1 harms, what each one can and can't tell you, and why the same drug can look terrifying in one dataset and totally clean in another.

TIER 1: THE RANDOMIZED TRIALS

This is the gold standard. Randomization kills confounding, so if the drug group has more of something, the drug probably did it.

The limit is sample size and time. A phase 3 obesity trial runs a few thousand people for a year or two. If a side effect happens in 1 out of every 10,000 people treated for a year, a trial that size will often catch ZERO cases, or one, which is statistically indistinguishable from bad luck. Trials are built to detect weight loss and A1c, not rare events.

So "no signal in the RCTs" does NOT mean "doesn't happen." It means "too rare for this study to see."

TIER 2: SPONTANEOUS REPORTING (FAERS AND FRIENDS)

This is where most scary headlines are born. Anyone can file an adverse event report. The database counts reports.

Here's the problem: there is no denominator. You know how many reports came in, you do NOT know how many people took the drug, and you have no idea what fraction of events got reported at all. Reporting also spikes when a drug is in the news or in litigation, so attention itself inflates the numbers.

Spontaneous reports are a smoke detector. They tell you where to go look. They are NOT an incidence rate, and anyone who turns them into one is doing something the system was never designed to do.

TIER 3: CLAIMS AND EHR DATABASES

Millions of patients, real-world use, and actual denominators. Much better. This is where most of the "2x risk" style numbers come from.

But the people who get prescribed a GLP-1 are diabetic, obese, hypertensive, and often have sleep apnea. They were already at higher risk for a lot of the bad outcomes being measured. That's confounding by indication, and statistical matching only partly fixes it. Add coding errors, people who filled a script and never injected it, and events that happened outside the health system, and you get a lot of noise around the signal.

TIER 4: CASE SERIES AND REFERRAL CENTERS

A specialty clinic notices a cluster of patients with the same problem and publishes it. Extremely valuable for generating a hypothesis. Terrible for estimating how common something is, because the sickest and weirdest cases are exactly who gets referred to a specialty clinic in the first place. Referral populations almost always inflate risk estimates.

TIER 5: META-ANALYSIS

Pooling studies narrows the confidence interval, which makes the answer look more precise. It does not fix the bias inside the studies you pooled. If you stack ten confounded observational studies, you get one very confident confounded answer.

THE BLIND SPOT NOBODY MEASURES

Every tier above is built on pharmacy-grade product, prescribed, in people with diabetes or obesity, under some kind of monitoring. If you're lean, running research-grade material, self-titrating, and never telling a doctor, you don't exist in ANY of these datasets. Not in the trials, not in the claims data, and usually not in the adverse event reports either. That's not me scaring you, that's just an honest statement of what the literature covers.

THE PATTERN

* RCTs: best at proving causation, blind to rare events
* Reports: best at spotting new signals, useless for frequency
* Big databases: best at frequency, worst at causation
* Case series: best at describing what the problem looks like, worst at how often

Notice that no single tier answers "how often does this happen to someone like me." That's why the reporting is unclear. It's not a cover-up, it's a structural gap in how drug safety data gets collected.

And this cuts both ways, by the way. Another headline this week links GLP-1 use to LOWER colorectal cancer risk in patients with inflammatory bowel disease. Same methodology, same confounding problems, opposite emotional reaction. If you're going to be skeptical of the scary association study, be equally skeptical of the flattering one.

WHAT THIS ACTUALLY MEANS FOR YOU

1. When you see a risk number, ask what tier it came from before you react.
2. Relative risk without absolute risk is a scare tactic. "Double the risk" of something that happens to 2 in 100,000 people is a very different conversation than double the risk of nausea.
3. Know the emergency signs for the stuff that actually lands people in the hospital: severe persistent abdominal pain radiating to the back, vomiting that won't stop, no bowel movements with a distended belly, sudden vision loss in one eye. Those are GO GET SEEN situations, not wait-it-out situations.
4. If something happens to you, report it. The system is passive, meaning it only knows what people bother to tell it. Under-reporting is a huge part of why the frequency question is unanswered.
5. Log your own stuff. Your personal n=1 record is the only dataset that's actually about you.

The real takeaway isn't that GLP-1s are secretly dangerous. It's that "we don't know how often" is a legitimate scientific answer, and it means you should be reading the methods section, not the headline.
