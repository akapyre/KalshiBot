# Registry Data Says Semaglutide Doesn't Cause Pancreatic Cancer

One of the top fears I get asked about with GLP-1s, right behind muscle loss, is cancer. New registry data just dropped saying semaglutide does NOT cause pancreatic cancer, so let's talk about where that fear came from and what registry data can and can't tell us.

WHERE THE FEAR STARTED

This is not a TikTok scare, it's an old one. Back in the early incretin era (the exenatide/sitagliptin days), adverse event reports started stacking up for pancreatitis in people on GLP-1 based drugs. Pancreatitis is a known risk factor for pancreatic cancer, so the logic chain people ran was simple: drug irritates pancreas, irritated pancreas turns into cancer. Add in the rodent thyroid C-cell tumor signal that put a boxed warning on this whole class, and you get a decade of people being scared of their pens.

The headline here is that new registry data does not support the pancreatic cancer part of that chain.

WHAT "REGISTRY DATA" ACTUALLY MEANS

This is the part I want you guys to understand, because it changes how much weight you put on the finding.

A registry study is not a randomized trial. Nobody was assigned semaglutide. Researchers take national or health-system records of real people, find who got the drug and who didn't, and count how many cancers showed up in each group over time.

* The good: huge numbers of real patients, years of follow-up, and rare outcomes like pancreatic cancer actually show up enough to count. Randomized trials are usually too small and too short to catch this.
* The bad: confounding. The people prescribed semaglutide are diabetic and obese, which is already a pancreatic cancer risk group. Same problem I broke down with the NAION eye stuff.

So registry data is exactly the right tool for a rare, slow cancer question, and it's still observational.

THE REVERSE CAUSATION TRAP

Here's the thing that makes pancreatic cancer specifically messy, and it's why this type of study has to be designed carefully.

Pancreatic cancer causes new-onset diabetes and unexplained weight loss YEARS before it's diagnosed. So a tumor that's already quietly growing can be what pushes someone into a diabetes diagnosis, which is what gets them the prescription in the first place. Then they get diagnosed with cancer 18 months later and it looks like the drug did it. The drug didn't do it. The cancer was there first, it just got to the finish line last.

Good registry analyses handle this with lag periods, basically throwing out the cases diagnosed in the first year or two. That's the single biggest thing separating a real finding from a scary correlation.

WHAT WE STILL DON'T KNOW

1. Pancreatic cancer is SLOW. Semaglutide hasn't been on the market long enough for truly long-horizon data. "No signal so far" is not the same as "no signal ever."
2. This is about pancreatic cancer, NOT pancreatitis. Acute pancreatitis is still a real, documented adverse event with this class and it's still on the label. Severe, persistent abdominal pain that bores into your back is a get-seen-now situation, not a wait-it-out one.
3. Almost all of this data comes from diabetic and obese patients on pharmacy-grade drug. It doesn't directly speak to lean people using research-grade sema, tirz or reta for body composition, and it says nothing about reta at all.

MY TAKE

This is another entry in a pattern we keep seeing with GLP-1s: a scary early hypothesis, then big real-world datasets showing the risk is much smaller or absent. It's genuinely reassuring, and it's also not permission to stop paying attention to your own body.
