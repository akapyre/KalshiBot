# Hot Take: A Headline Is NOT A Study

You've probably seen the headline floating around this week: an "Ozempic rival" delivering 25% weight loss in a phase 3 trial. And I guarantee most of the people sharing it stopped reading at the number. So let's talk about it.

Read that headline again. It literally says "at the highest dose." That detail is sitting right there in the title, and people still screenshot it, post it, and treat 25% like it's the baseline everyone gets. It's not. It's the top of the dose range, in a selected trial population, over a set duration, with dropouts and side effect rates attached that the headline never shows you.

Here's the part that might sting a little.

1. A headline is the marketing of a study, not the study.
The number in the title is always the most impressive number they found. That's the job of a headline. Your job is to go find out who it happened to and under what conditions.

2. "Phase 3" tells you the stage, not the story.
It means it's in a large human trial. Good! That's real evidence, and way better than a rodent study. But phase 3 topline results are usually averages, and averages hide a HUGE spread of individual responses.

3. Half of what hits your feed isn't journalism at all.
Scroll any peptide news feed right now and you'll see press releases dressed up as "2026 reviews" sitting right next to actual pharma reporting. Same font, same layout, totally different purpose. One is reporting a result. The other is selling you something. If you can't tell which one you're reading, you're going to get played.

4. Mainstream attention is NOT the same as validated quality.
Pharmacy Times just ran a piece on exactly this as peptides go mainstream, that standards and documentation matter more than ever now that everyone's involved. Popularity is not a safety study. More people using something just means more people using something.

I'm not telling you to be cynical about everything. I'm telling you to add about 90 seconds to your process. Click the article. Find the actual trial. Ask what the dose was, how long it ran, who the participants were, and what the dropout rate looked like. If you can't find that, you don't have a result, you have an advertisement.

If you've been guilty of this, I'm not mad at you. I was there too, we all were. Just slow down before the next number makes you reach for your wallet.
