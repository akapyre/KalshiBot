# Hot Take: Access Was Never Your Real Problem

This one might sting a little, so brace yourself.

Right now there are op-eds flying in both directions about peptide access. One from the Reason Foundation says RFK Jr. is right to expand consumer access to peptides. One from the Center for Science in the Public Interest says FDA moves on peptides are putting consumers in harm's way. Same week, opposite conclusions, both written with total confidence.

And here's my hot take: for most of you, ACCESS was never the bottleneck. Understanding was.

You can already get almost anything. That's been true for years. More access doesn't make you better at this, it just shortens the distance between "I saw a post about it" and "it's in my fridge." The part that actually protects you is the part nobody wants to do, which is sitting down and learning what the molecule does before it's in your body.

So let's talk about what that actually looks like.

1. Know the EVIDENCE TIER, not the vibe.

There's a new free guide called Peplogue that grades peptides on evidence and shows the legal status by country. I think that's a great idea, and it also quietly exposes something: most people running a compound cannot tell you whether it has phase 3 human data, one small open-label trial, or nothing but rodents. If you can't answer that about what you're running right now, that's the gap.

2. Know the DIFFERENCE between "legal" and "studied."

Regulatory status and scientific evidence are two separate lanes. A compound can be perfectly legal to obtain and still have almost zero human data. A compound can have great human data and still be restricted where you live. Those two facts don't comment on each other at all.

3. Know who's writing the thing you're reading.

A think tank op-ed, a consumer advocacy op-ed, and a PubMed abstract are three different animals. Op-eds are arguments. Abstracts are data with limitations attached. Both have value, but ONLY one of them is telling you what actually happened in a study.

4. Know that "more available" means more noise.

When access expands, marketing expands faster than education does. Every wave of new availability comes with a wave of confident people explaining compounds they've never read a paper on. That's not a conspiracy, that's just how attention works.

I'm not anti-access. I genuinely think adults should be able to make informed decisions about their own bodies. The word doing the heavy lifting in that sentence is INFORMED.

So my ask is simple. Pick the one compound you're most curious about right now and go find out what tier of evidence it actually sits on. Rodent? Cell culture? Small human trial? Big trial? That single question will change how you approach this more than any policy headline will.
