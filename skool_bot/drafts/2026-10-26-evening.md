# Is A GLP-1 Quietly Leaving You Protein And Vitamin D Deficient?

You've probably seen the headlines this week saying Ozempic users may be walking around protein and vitamin D deficient, so let's talk about it.

Short version up front: this is a REAL concern, but it's not the drug chemically stealing nutrients from you. It's a math problem. And the honest thing I have to say before anything else is that what's circulating right now is reporting and expert commentary, NOT a paper with effect sizes attached. So I'm going to walk you through what we actually have, and I am NOT going to make up numbers to fill the gaps.

WHAT'S ACTUALLY ON THE TABLE

1. NewsNation (Oct 2026) - experts warning that people on semaglutide may face protein and vitamin D deficiencies. This is expert warning, not a trial readout. No incidence rate, no cohort size, no control group. Treat it as a flag, not a finding.

2. Katherine R. Tuttle, MD, FASN on semaglutide in type 2 diabetes and CKD (American Journal of Managed Care, Oct 2026) - a nephrology discussion of GLP-1 use in people who already have kidney disease. Why this matters to the deficiency conversation: your kidney is where vitamin D gets converted into its active form. Kidney function and vitamin D status are already linked before a GLP-1 ever enters the picture.

3. CagriSema in women across menopause stages and hormone therapy use (Drug Topics, Oct 2026) - a subgroup look at weight reduction in women at different menopausal stages. The headline tells us weight came down across those groups. It does NOT tell us anything about body composition or micronutrients, and I'm not going to pretend it does. I'm flagging it because postmenopausal women are the exact population where low protein intake and low vitamin D stack on top of existing bone loss risk.

THE MECHANISM, SIMPLY

Think of your daily food intake as a bucket. A GLP-1 doesn't poke holes in the bucket, it just hands you a much smaller cup.

* Appetite drops hard, so total intake drops hard
* Protein is usually the first casualty, because meat and whole-food protein feel heavy when gastric emptying is slowed
* Vitamin D is fat-soluble, so when fat intake falls off a cliff, so does your dietary intake of it
* Nausea pushes people toward bland, soft, low-protein food

That's it. It's an intake story, not an absorption-blocking story. Which is also why it's FIXABLE in a way most drug side effects aren't.

THE PATTERN

Every one of these threads points the same direction: the compound is doing its job, and the diet around it is the weak link. Fast weight loss with low protein is how you lose lean mass instead of just fat. Low vitamin D plus low protein plus rapid loss is the combination that worries people looking at bone health, especially in older and postmenopausal populations.

Notice what we DON'T have here. We don't have a controlled trial measuring serum vitamin D before and after in GLP-1 users with numbers I can quote you. That paper may exist, but it isn't what these headlines are reporting on. Expert concern and proven deficiency rates are two different evidence levels, and you should hold them differently.

WHAT THIS ACTUALLY MEANS FOR YOU

1. Protein first, every single meal. When the cup gets smaller, what goes in it matters more, not less.
2. Get bloodwork that includes vitamin D if you've been losing weight fast for months. Guessing is not a strategy.
3. Resistance train. Protein intake without a stimulus to use it is wasted.
4. If you have kidney disease or you're postmenopausal, this is a conversation to have with an actual doctor, not a comment section.

Nobody needs to panic over this one. Undereating protein on a GLP-1 is one of the most preventable problems in the entire space, you just have to be intentional about it.
