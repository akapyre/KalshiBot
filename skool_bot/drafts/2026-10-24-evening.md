# Half-Life Explained: Why Week 5 Hits Different Than Week 1

One of the most common things I get asked is some version of "I've been on this for three weeks and it feels stronger now than when I started, did something change?" Nothing changed. That's just half-life and steady state doing their thing, so let's talk about it.

THE GYM FLOOR ANALOGY

Picture your bloodstream as a gym floor with ONE door.

Every dose is a group of people walking in. Your liver and kidneys are the staff at the door, kicking people out at a fixed PERCENTAGE per hour, not a fixed number. That's the part people miss. Clearance isn't "10 people leave per hour." It's "half the room clears out every X hours."

That X is the half-life.

* Short half-life compound = staff throwing people out fast. Room is basically empty by tomorrow.
* Long half-life compound = staff moving slow. Yesterday's crowd is STILL in there when the next group walks in.

And that's the whole trick. When the new group arrives before the old group leaves, the room gets fuller. Then fuller. Then fuller.

STEADY STATE

Eventually the room gets crowded enough that the percentage being kicked out equals the number walking in. Now the gym floor stops filling. It just cycles. That's steady state, and the rule of thumb across pharmacology is that it takes roughly 4 to 5 half-lives to get there.

This is why a once-weekly compound with a week-long half-life doesn't show you its real self until about a month in. Your week 1 blood level is NOT the same blood level as your week 5, even on the identical dose. Same dose, bigger crowd.

It also explains three things people constantly misread:

1. "The side effects came back and I didn't change anything." You didn't need to. The floor was still filling.
2. "I stopped and still felt it for weeks." Long half-life means the room empties slowly too. Clearing out takes about the same 4 to 5 half-lives on the way down.
3. "I missed one and nothing happened." On a long half-life compound, one missing group doesn't empty a packed room. On a short half-life daily peptide, it basically does, which is why daily stuff feels so much more all-or-nothing.

WHY THIS MATTERS WHEN YOU SWITCH OR RESTART

There's a case report that just dropped in Cureus describing a small bowel obstruction temporally associated with an UNSUPERVISED switch from tirzepatide to semaglutide after a prolonged treatment interruption. I want to be clear about what that is and isn't: it's a single patient, n=1, and "temporally associated" means it happened around the same time, NOT that causation was proven.

But the setup is exactly the scenario half-life math warns you about. A long gap means the gym floor emptied. Your gut tolerance isn't where you left it. Stacking a different molecule on top of a partially cleared one is a situation nobody's blood levels are predictable in. That's a conversation for whoever is actually managing your care, not something to eyeball.

THE TAKEAWAY

Half-life isn't trivia. It's the single number that tells you how long a compound takes to show up, how long it takes to leave, and how forgiving a missed dose is going to be. Learn the half-life of anything you're researching before you learn anything else about it.

If something feels different in week 5 than week 1, you're not crazy and you're not broken. The room just got full.
