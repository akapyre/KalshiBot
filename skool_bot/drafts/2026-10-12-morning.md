# Oral Semaglutide Crossed 100K Scripts A Month - Here's What That Pill Actually Does

You've probably seen the "GLP-1 in a pill" headlines going around, and now there's a number attached to it. Medscape is reporting oral semaglutide for obesity topped 100,000 prescriptions PER MONTH within two months of being available. That's one of the fastest ramps we've seen in this space, so let's talk about what's actually in that tablet and why it's not just "Ozempic you swallow."

THE PROBLEM WITH SWALLOWING A PEPTIDE

Semaglutide is a peptide. Your stomach exists specifically to destroy peptides. Pepsin and acid chew up amino acid chains before they ever reach the bloodstream, which is the whole reason peptides got stuck as injectables in the first place.

So the pill isn't just semaglutide. It's semaglutide paired with an absorption enhancer called SNAC. Think of SNAC as a bodyguard that walks the drug through a bad neighborhood. It buffers the pH right around the tablet as it dissolves against the stomach wall, which protects the peptide from being shredded, and it temporarily helps it slip across the gastric lining into circulation.

It works. It just doesn't work efficiently. Oral bioavailability is roughly 1%, meaning the overwhelming majority of what you swallow never makes it in. That's why the oral milligram numbers look so much bigger than injectable milligram numbers. Same molecule, totally different math. A mg on a tablet and a mg in a syringe are NOT the same unit of effect.

WHY THE INSTRUCTIONS ARE SO ANNOYING

This is the part people underestimate:

* It has to be taken fasted, first thing, with only a small sip of plain water
* You wait before eating, drinking, or taking other oral meds
* Food, coffee, or too much water in the stomach dilutes the SNAC effect and tanks absorption

With an injection, absorption is pretty much absorption. With the pill, YOUR ROUTINE is part of the dose. Someone who takes it with breakfast and someone who takes it fasted are not running the same protocol, even on the same tablet.

WHAT THIS NUMBER ACTUALLY TELLS US

100K+ scripts a month tells us about DEMAND and access, not superiority. It tells us a lot of people would rather swallow something than stick themselves, and that needle aversion has been a bigger barrier to this category than most of us assumed.

What it does NOT tell us:

1. How many of those people stay on it past month three
2. How tolerability compares head to head in the real world (GI side effects are still driven by the same receptor, just a different delivery route)
3. How many are switching off injections vs. starting fresh
4. Whether adherence to the fasted-water-wait routine holds up outside a trial setting

I'd also point out the other headline sitting next to this one: Lilly is already fighting a black market for its new obesity drug before it even launches. Oral delivery makes these compounds easier to ship, easier to fake, and easier to sell to people who never talk to a prescriber. A pill feels casual in a way a vial never does, and that's exactly the psychology that gets people hurt.

BOTTOM LINE

The pill is a genuine pharmacology win. Getting a peptide through the stomach at all is impressive engineering. But it's the same receptor, the same side effect profile, and the same titration rules, with an extra absorption variable bolted on top.
