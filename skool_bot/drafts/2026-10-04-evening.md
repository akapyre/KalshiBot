# Surgery or Sedation While You're On a GLP-1? Here's Your Checklist

A headline went around this week from Anesthesiology (via Medical Xpress) reporting that pulmonary aspiration and regurgitation under anesthesia are MORE likely in people on GLP-1 receptor agonists. It's one of the few GLP-1 risks that's almost entirely preventable with a conversation, so let's talk about how to actually handle it.

First, the mechanism in plain English. GLP-1s slow gastric emptying. That's a big part of why you feel full for hours. The problem is that anesthesia relaxes everything, including the valve at the top of your stomach. If there's still food or fluid sitting there when you go under, it can come back up and go into your lungs. That's aspiration, and it's a genuinely dangerous complication. It's NOT that the drug is poisoning you, it's that your stomach is on a slower clock than the standard fasting rules assume.

THE CHECKLIST

1. Tell them. Every single time.
Surgery, colonoscopy, endoscopy, dental sedation, even "twilight" sedation for something minor. The anesthesia team needs to know you're on a GLP-1 BEFORE the day of, not while you're on the table.

2. Say the compound name, not "a weight loss shot."
Semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide. If you're running a research-grade compound it won't show up in your pharmacy record, so you have to say it out loud. Nobody is going to call the cops. The anesthesiologist just needs to know what's in you so they can plan the airway.

3. Ask the anesthesia team about timing, and let THEM make the call.
Some centers want these held before a procedure, some want a longer fast, some use ultrasound to check if your stomach is empty. That's their decision based on your procedure and your compound, not something you should guess at off a Reddit thread.

4. Follow the fasting instructions exactly, and ask if they want to extend them.
Standard pre-op fasting windows were written for people with normal gastric emptying. Ask directly: "Do you want me on clear liquids longer because of this?" That one question does a lot of work.

5. Report your GI symptoms honestly in the days before.
Still getting nausea? Burping up food hours after eating? Feeling full the next morning? That's a sign your stomach is emptying slowly RIGHT NOW, and it's exactly the info the team needs.

6. Get it in the chart, not just in conversation.
Tell the scheduler, tell pre-op nursing, tell the anesthesiologist. Things get lost between three different departments. Repeat yourself.

7. Plan the recovery side too.
Appetite is already suppressed and now you're adding post-op nausea meds and reduced activity. Hydration and protein become the whole game while you heal, and muscle is the thing you least want to lose sitting on the couch.

ONE MORE THING

This applies to way more than big surgeries. Dental work with sedation, a scope, an ER procedure. If someone is going to make you unconscious or deeply sedated, they need to know.

I don't want anyone reading this and panicking about their next appointment. The risk signal is real but it's manageable, and it's managed with information. The people who get in trouble are the ones who didn't mention it.

If you've had a procedure while running a GLP-1 and your team did something smart about it, drop it below. That stuff helps people.
