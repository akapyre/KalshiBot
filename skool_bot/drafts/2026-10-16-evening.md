# How To Get Bloodwork That Actually Means Something

In the top three questions I get, one is always "should I get bloodwork?" The answer is always yes. But the part nobody talks about is that HOW you get the draw decides whether those numbers are useful or just noise. So let's talk about it.

A blood panel is a single frame from a movie. If you shoot that frame right after leg day, dehydrated, at a random time of day, on a random lab, you're going to look at the picture and panic over something that was never real.

Here's how I'd approach it.

1. Get a BASELINE before you start anything.

This is the one people skip and regret. If you pull labs for the first time six months into a protocol, you have no idea what's the compound and what's just you. A pre-start panel turns every future draw into a comparison instead of a guess.

2. Control the variables so the only thing changing is you.

* Same lab, same panel, same time of day when you can
* Fasted if your lipids and glucose are part of the picture
* Hydrated — being dry concentrates your blood and can nudge hematocrit, creatinine and electrolytes up
* Normal sleep the night before, not a 4-hour night

3. Do NOT train hard right before the draw.

Heavy lifting beats up muscle tissue and dumps creatine kinase into your blood. CK is a big one, but AST and ALT also leak out of muscle, not just liver. People see elevated liver enzymes after a brutal training week and spiral, when the real story was squats. Give yourself a couple of easy days before the stick.

4. Keep the timing relative to your protocol CONSISTENT.

You don't need to engineer anything here, you just need to be repeatable. If draw #1 happens at one point in your week and draw #2 happens at a totally different point, you're comparing two different movies. Pick a slot in your routine and stay in it.

5. Pull a panel that's actually wide enough to be useful.

A finger-poke glucose isn't a health check. The usual backbone people work with:

* CBC (red cells, white cells, hematocrit)
* CMP (kidney, liver, electrolytes, glucose)
* Lipid panel
* A1c and fasting insulin
* Thyroid panel
* Full hormone panel if that's relevant to what you're running
* IGF-1 if you're touching anything in the GH space

6. Read the TREND, not the data point.

One value outside the reference range is a question, not a diagnosis. Three draws walking the same direction is a signal. Put them in a spreadsheet side by side, oldest to newest, and look at the slope. That's where the actual information lives.

7. Write down what was going on around the draw.

Sleep, training, hydration, illness, how long you fasted, what you're running. Six months from now you will not remember, and context is the entire difference between "my kidneys are failing" and "I was dehydrated and trained the day before."

8. Take it to a real doctor.

You can learn to read your own labs, and you should. But you are not the person who should be interpreting a genuinely weird result on your own. Bring the trend, bring the notes, let someone who does this for a living tell you what it means.

Bloodwork is the only honest feedback loop you have in this space. The scale lies, the mirror lies, and how you FEEL lies the most. Get the baseline, control the variables, and track it over time.
