# Humanin - The Peptide Your Mitochondria Write Themselves

You've probably seen humanin floating around in longevity circles lately and written it off as another "mitochondrial health" buzzword. It's actually one of the weirdest, coolest discoveries in peptide biology, so let's talk about it.

WHAT IT IS

Humanin is a 24-amino-acid peptide, but here's the part that makes it strange: it isn't coded in your nuclear DNA like almost every other protein in your body. It's coded inside the mitochondrial genome, in a stretch of the 16S rRNA gene. Your mitochondria literally write it themselves. It was the first of what we now call mitochondrial-derived peptides (MDPs), a family that also includes MOTS-c and the SHLP series.

HISTORY

It was discovered back in 2001 by a Japanese group that was screening surviving brain tissue from an Alzheimer's patient, asking why certain neurons made it while the ones around them died. They pulled out this little peptide and named it humanin. That's the origin story, it was found because it was protecting cells that should have been dead.

Status: NOT FDA approved for anything. Not scheduled. It's a research compound, full stop.

THE MECHANISM (SIMPLE VERSION)

Think of your mitochondria as the power plants inside the cell. When a cell takes too much damage, there's a built-in demolition process called apoptosis, programmed cell death. A protein called BAX is basically the demolition crew. It travels to the mitochondrial membrane, punches holes in it, and the cell tears itself down.

Humanin is the power plant sending a distress text to the demolition crew that says "stand down, we're still working in here." It binds BAX and keeps it from docking on the mitochondria. The crew never shows up, the cell survives the hit.

It also signals OUTWARD through receptors on the cell surface, including FPR2 and a three-part receptor complex (CNTFR / WSX-1 / gp130), which is how one mitochondria's distress call ends up being heard by other tissues. That's the big conceptual shift here, mitochondria aren't just batteries, they're sending hormone-like signals around the body.

WHAT RESEARCHERS ARE INTERESTED IN

* Neuroprotection, this is where it started, cell models of amyloid-beta toxicity
* Insulin sensitivity and metabolic signaling
* Cardiac and endothelial protection in ischemia models
* Aging, circulating humanin levels decline as people get older, which is why the longevity crowd latched onto it

There are also more potent synthetic analogs (the S14G version, usually called HNG) that show up all over the rodent literature because native humanin is fragile and short-lived.

WHAT TO WATCH FOR

Honestly? The biggest thing to watch for is the hype. A peptide whose entire job is telling cells "don't die" sounds incredible until you remember that controlled cell death is also how your body clears damaged and precancerous cells. Chronically telling everything to survive is not automatically a win, and researchers have raised that exact question. There's no long-term human safety data to answer it.

EVIDENCE LEVEL

This is the part people skip. Humanin is overwhelmingly cell culture and rodent work. The human data that exists is mostly observational, measuring blood levels of humanin in different age groups and disease states, not giving it to people and tracking outcomes. There is no large human trial establishing that administering humanin does anything in a healthy lifter.

So file it under "fascinating biology, very early." The reason I think it's worth knowing is the concept, your mitochondria are an endocrine organ that talks back. That reframe is going to drive a lot of the next decade of longevity research whether or not humanin itself ends up being the compound that matters.
