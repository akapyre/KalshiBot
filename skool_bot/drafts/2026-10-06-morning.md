# Your eGFR Might Be Lying To You On A GLP-1

A headline crossed my feed today from Medscape: "Your Patient Lost Weight on a GLP-1. Can You Trust the eGFR?" That is a question that matters a LOT to lifters, and most people have never thought about it, so let's talk about it.

WHAT eGFR ACTUALLY IS

eGFR stands for estimated glomerular filtration rate. It's the number on your bloodwork that's supposed to tell you how well your kidneys are filtering. The key word there is ESTIMATED. Nobody measured your kidney filtration directly. A lab plugged your creatinine, age and sex into an equation and spit out a number.

So what's creatinine? It's a waste product from creatine phosphate breaking down in your muscles. Your muscle tissue is basically a creatinine factory running 24/7. More muscle, more creatinine in the blood, all else equal.

And here's the part that trips people up: the equation assumes high creatinine means your kidneys aren't clearing it. It has no idea whether you're a 250lb powerlifter or a sedentary 140lb guy. It just sees the number.

WHY THIS GETS WEIRD ON A GLP-1

Think of it like judging how fast water drains out of a tub by measuring how much water is left, without knowing how much anyone poured in.

* If you lose a meaningful chunk of LEAN mass on a GLP-1, you're producing less creatinine. Creatinine drops. The equation reads that as "kidneys got better" and your eGFR goes UP, even if nothing changed in your kidneys.
* The flip side is just as real. Appetite suppression means less food and less fluid. Dehydration and lower blood volume can push creatinine up and make an eGFR look worse than your kidneys actually are.
* And if you're a lifter carrying a lot of muscle, or supplementing creatine (which raises serum creatinine on its own), your baseline eGFR may already read artificially low. That's not kidney damage, that's a measurement problem.

So on a GLP-1 you can have an eGFR that moves in either direction for reasons that have NOTHING to do with your kidneys.

WHAT I'D KEEP IN MIND

1. One lab value is a data point, not a diagnosis. Trends over months beat a single draw.
2. Cystatin C is the other marker clinicians use for this exact problem. It's not produced by muscle, so it's much less affected by how jacked you are or how much lean mass you just lost. If your creatinine-based eGFR looks strange, that's the conversation to have with your doctor.
3. Hydration status at the time of the draw matters. Appetite suppression plus a fasted morning draw is not a neutral snapshot.
4. Protect lean mass. This is the recurring theme of basically every GLP-1 conversation right now. Training hard and eating enough protein doesn't just keep you looking good, it keeps your bloodwork interpretable.
5. Real kidney issues don't announce themselves through one eGFR blip. Persistent changes, protein in the urine, swelling, big drops in output, those get a doctor, not a Reddit thread.

WHAT WE STILL DON'T KNOW

This is a clinical commentary framing a measurement question, NOT a trial showing GLP-1s hurt or help kidneys. The large outcome trials have generally been favorable for kidney endpoints in diabetics, but how to interpret creatinine in someone who dropped 40lb of total body weight, some of it lean, is a genuinely unsettled problem. And none of it has been studied in research-grade users who are lean to begin with and lifting heavy.
